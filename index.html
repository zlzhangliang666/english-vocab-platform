Why Cities Sound Quieter at Night

B1 · 说明文8 个目标词

Cities are not actually silent at night. They simply trade the noise of daytime for another frequency. The low hum of air-conditioning units, the distant hiss of tyres on a motorway, the compressor behind a convenience-store fridge — none of these sounds is harsh on its own. Together they form a sustained layer of background noise.

Researchers call this the urban ambient soundscape. Unlike a sudden horn, it is rarely noticeable, so most people stop hearing it within a few weeks. That adaptation is useful in the short term. In the long term, however, constant exposure keeps the nervous system slightly alert, and it becomes harder to concentrate on a book or fall asleep.

The effect is not evenly spread. People who live near main roads absorb more low-frequency sound than those on quiet side streets, and the difference shows up in sleep studies. A recent survey found that residents of noisy blocks fell asleep about twenty minutes later on average, even when they reported feeling used to the sound.

What helps is not silence but contrast. A small fountain, a line of trees, or a bedroom facing a courtyard can break the steady layer into irregular pieces. The brain notices change far more easily than constant pressure, and that small variation is often enough to let attention settle.

So the next time a city night feels calm, it may be worth listening more carefully. The quiet is not the absence of sound. It is a texture we have learned to stop noticing — and one we can still decide to change.
